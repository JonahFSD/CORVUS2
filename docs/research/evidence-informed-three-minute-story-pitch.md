# Evidence-informed design for Osanwe's two-minute story pitch

Last reviewed: 2026-08-27

## Decision

How should Osanwe use three to four slides and a hard two-minute cutoff to make the product understandable, credible, and memorable without burying the human outcome under unvalidated technical claims?

The working Round 2 constraint is now a **hard two-minute cutoff**, with slides welcome and emphasis on the pitch, story, and idea. This note treats selection by that panel as the immediate outcome. It does not assume that a compelling pitch establishes product efficacy.

## Bottom line

The most defensible format is a **four-beat causal story about one clearly labeled composite person**, with the product named and explained in plain language during the opening beat:

1. a person discloses distress to an AI;
2. the conversation ends without a workable route to human care;
3. Osanwe turns only what the person approves into an editable manifest and offers vetted therapist matches; and
4. the chosen therapist receives approved context so the first session can begin with relevant context rather than from zero.

That design is a **new proposal**, not a scientifically proven winning pitch. It is supported indirectly by experiments on narrative transportation, character identification, causal structure, and multimedia learning, plus first-party TED and Y Combinator presentation guidance. None of those sources tests this product, this panel, or a two-minute mental-health pitch.

The presentation should make one bounded promise: **Osanwe reduces the work between an AI disclosure and a context-rich first conversation with a suitable therapist.** It should not claim to diagnose, select a “perfect” therapist, prevent suicide, infer a person's true inner state, or prove that therapy will work.

## Evidence ledger

| Material claim | Status | Origin | Evidence and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Absorption in a narrative can affect story-consistent beliefs and evaluations. | causally supported under the reported experimental conditions | primary study | Four experiments by Green and Brock manipulated or measured “transportation” while participants read narratives; greater transportation was associated with more story-consistent beliefs and favorable character evaluations. The stimuli were written stories, not live startup pitches, so transfer to Round 2 is a hypothesis. [Green & Brock (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701) |
| A character's perspective can influence identification and attitudes. | causally supported under the reported experimental conditions | primary study | Two experiments varied which opposing character narrated a written story; perspective changed identification, and identification mediated story-consistent attitudes. This supports staying with one point of view, but it does not establish that a fictional protagonist wins pitches. [de Graaf et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650211408594) |
| Events tied into a causal chain are more likely to be recalled and judged important. | observed/model-supported, author-reported | primary study | Causal-chain membership and number of causal connections explained unique variance in recall, summarization, and judged importance in reanalyses of existing story data. The study was not a randomized pitch comparison. [Trabasso & van den Broek (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(85)90049-X) |
| Adding redundant written text to spoken/visual material can impair learning. | causally supported under the reported experimental conditions | primary study | Across four experiments on a narrated animation, concurrent on-screen text that summarized or duplicated narration reduced retention or transfer, and interesting but irrelevant details also impaired performance. This was an instructional task with college students, not a stage pitch. [Mayer, Heiser & Lonn (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.1.187) |
| A sentence assertion plus visual evidence can outperform common topic-and-bullet slides for comprehension. | causally supported under the reported experimental conditions | primary study | In a comparison involving 110 engineering students hearing the same technical narration, assertion-evidence slides produced better comprehension, fewer misconceptions, lower perceived cognitive load, and stronger delayed recall than common-practice slides. Generalization from engineering instruction to a pitch remains untested. [Garner & Alley (2013)](https://www.writing.engr.psu.edu/research.html) |
| “Use one named victim” is not a reliable law of persuasion. | uncertain/mixed | primary replication and reanalysis | A preregistered conceptual replication found no identifiable-victim effect on hypothetical donations, and its publication-bias-adjusted reanalysis called the literature into question. Use a single protagonist to maintain a coherent causal point of view, not because naming a person is guaranteed to increase support. [Maier, Wong & Feldman (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.90203) |
| Narrative evidence is not generally more persuasive than statistical evidence. | mixed across contexts | primary meta-analysis | A meta-analysis of 50 studies (65 experimental comparisons; 13,113 participants) found no overall persuasive advantage for narrative or statistical evidence, with effects varying by context and outcome. The story still needs a clear, plausible product mechanism; emotional identification cannot substitute for one. [Xu (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1080/10410236.2022.2137750) |
| Simple slides, early product clarity, emotional restraint, and extensive rehearsal are established practitioner guidance. | specified guidance, not causal evidence | first-party practitioner sources | TED advises one point per slide, little text, conversational delivery, and repeated rehearsal with the actual slide control; its organizer guidance specifically flags forced emotion, overstatement, utopianism, dystopianism, and purely anecdotal evidence as problems to remove. YC advises explaining the product clearly and early, walking through the user path, eliminating jargon, making one point per slide, and testing whether another person can explain the company back. YC's short-pitch tool treats slides as scenery for the speaker and recommends elevating roughly three memorable “vertebrae.” These are first-party practices, not controlled proof of pitch success. ## Recommended four-slide spine

Each heading below is a complete assertion. The body should be one full-bleed visual or a very small product mockup, not a second script. “Maya” must be described as a **composite illustration**, not a real outcome or testimonial.

| Beat | Slide assertion and visual | Spoken work | Target time |
|---|---|---|---:|
| 1. Disclosure | **“At 1:47 a.m., Maya asks an AI for help—but the conversation ends where care should begin.”** One restrained image: a person and a lit phone, with “1:47 a.m.” visible. | Open inside one concrete moment, then say plainly what Osanwe does within the first 20 seconds. The story earns attention; product clarity earns the next minute. | 0:00–0:27 |
| 2. Friction | **“For Maya, getting human help still means starting over.”** A single visual path: search results → availability/payment → intake forms → repeated retelling. | Name only the frictions the product actually addresses. The turn is: Maya has already done the emotionally difficult disclosure, but the system asks her to begin again. | 0:27–0:50 |
| 3. Product | **“With Maya's permission, Osanwe turns what she already shared into an editable manifest—and brings her vetted matches.”** A compact three-step mockup: approve sources → review/correct manifest → choose among matches. | This is the longest and most concrete beat. Explain the user path, not the architecture: Maya chooses what enters the manifest, reviews it, and chooses among eligible therapists. | 0:50–1:28 |
| 4. Human handoff | **“Her therapist starts with context. Maya starts with a human.”** Show the therapist viewing only the approved summary, then the two people in a first conversation. | Resolve the exact opening problem. AI exits center stage; the human relationship begins. Close on the bounded thesis: “AI should not become the relationship. It should make reaching the right human radically easier.” | 1:28–1:53 |

The remaining seven seconds are a cutoff buffer, not permission to add another idea. The stage script should be timed to the presenter's actual delivery; a nominal word count cannot guarantee compliance with a hard cutoff. If the competition requires a direct ask, substitute it for an existing final sentence rather than adding time.

### Three-slide fallback

If four visual changes make delivery brittle, combine slides 1 and 2. Do **not** combine the manifest and therapist handoff: those are different causal mechanisms, and collapsing them makes the product look like an unexplained matching service.

## Delivery design

These are prospective recommendations, not established effects for this pitch.

- Speak in the present tense and remain with Maya's point of view until the final thesis. Do not interrupt her arc with market size, model names, or architecture.
- Treat the emotional sequence as **recognition → friction → agency → relief**, not escalating tragedy. The audience should feel the waste of having finally said the hard thing and then being asked to start over. Do not use suicide as dramatic leverage, perform Maya's distress, or imply that this product has prevented deaths.
- Pause briefly after the opening sentence, before the product reveal, and before the closing line. Do not perform distress with theatrical intensity.
- Use ordinary verbs: “chooses,” “reviews,” “corrects,” “matches,” and “shares.” Avoid “latent state,” “hyper-personalization,” “digital twin,” “download her brain,” and “perfect match.”
- Draft roughly 220–250 spoken words, then time the actual delivery and cut until three consecutive conversational runs finish by 1:53. This word range is only a drafting heuristic; the speaker's measured cadence is authoritative.
- Memorize the four transitions and the exact final two sentences. Rehearse the rest until conversational rather than reading from the slides.
- Rehearse standing, with the actual deck and clicker, through at least three consecutive timed runs. Record and watch one run without sound for body movement and one audio-only for pace and clarity.

## Evidence-first rehearsal evaluation

Treat the pitch as a falsifiable communication artifact: define the intended audience takeaway, compare it with the simplest clear explanation, and change one major element between rehearsal rounds.

### Outcome and baseline

- **Outcome:** a naive listener can accurately explain the person, problem, product mechanism, control boundary, and human outcome after one viewing.
- **Baseline:** the existing technical Corvus pitch or a plain two-sentence explanation, whichever is the current simplest comparator.
- **Candidate mechanisms:** (A) causal narrative improves coherent recall; (B) early product clarity, rather than narrative, does most of the work; (C) the story generates emotion but reduces credibility because the product appears hand-wavy.

### Smallest informative rehearsal test

Show each pitch once to separate naive listeners who are not already immersed in Osanwe. Do not tell them which version is preferred. Ask for unaided playback before soliciting opinions:

1. “What does Osanwe do?”
2. “What changed for Maya?”
3. “What information does the therapist receive, and who controls it?”
4. “What did the pitch claim that the product can do?”
5. “What is the one thing you still do not believe?”

Record answers verbatim. Also record duration, cutoff risk, every term that required clarification, and whether listeners incorrectly inferred diagnosis, guaranteed matching, crisis response, or therapy replacement.

### Prospective pass and revision rules

| Gate | Pass rule for the next rehearsal draft | If it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Product clarity | At least 4 of 5 naive listeners independently mention both the user-approved manifest and therapist matching/handoff. | Put the plain product sentence earlier; remove metaphor before adding explanation. |
| Causal coherence | At least 4 of 5 can retell disclosure → friction → manifest/match → human session in order. | Remove side claims and restore a single causal chain. |
| Boundary fidelity | No listener reports that Osanwe diagnoses, chooses perfectly, guarantees treatment success, or replaces the therapist. | Replace the wording that created the inference; do not repair it with fine print. |
| Retention | After ten minutes, at least 4 of 5 retain the human-handoff idea and at least 3 retain the manifest. | Strengthen slide assertions and visual evidence; cut competing concepts. |
| Delivery | Three consecutive live runs finish between 1:45 and 1:53 with correct slide cues, audible pauses, and no rushed ending. | Cut words. Do not accelerate delivery. |

These thresholds are **specified acceptance criteria**, not validated psychometric cutoffs. Five listeners can expose confusion; they cannot estimate competition win probability. Change one major element between rehearsal rounds so the source of improvement remains interpretable.

## What belongs in Q&A, not the two-minute deck

Prepare one truthful sentence for each topic, but do not add slides unless the organizers require them:

- **Why not an ordinary AI chat?** It can converse, but it does not by itself provide consented, correctable intake, verified therapist supply, or accountable human handoff.
- **What is the ML?** The LLM structures user-approved information; matching should begin with explicit eligibility and preference rules, and additional model complexity must beat that baseline prospectively.
- **What makes a therapist vetted?** Answer only with the checks and operational process actually specified or implemented; do not imply a proof of quality.
- **Is it therapy or crisis care?** No. It is an intake and routing tool and must present the appropriate emergency pathway separately.
- **How do you know it helps?** At present, the pitch specifies the intended workflow and measurable product outcome. It should not represent clinical benefit as observed until prospective evidence exists.

## Unknowns

- No reviewed study establishes that four slides are better than three for this pitch.
- No reviewed study validates “Maya,” this emotional arc, or the proposed closing line with the Round 2 judges.
- Narrative engagement may improve recall while reducing perceived rigor if the product mechanism is vague.
- The present record does not establish user demand, therapist adoption, match quality, reduced time to care, or improved clinical outcomes. Those are separate product and scientific questions.
