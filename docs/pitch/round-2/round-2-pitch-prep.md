## The pitch

Target: **235–250 spoken words**, finishing around 1:55 at a deliberate pace.

### Slide 1 — Your behavior is already being modeled

> Your behavior is already being modeled. The objective just is not your flourishing.
>
> Technology companies learn which signals predict a click, purchase, or another minute on-screen. I want to turn that system around: a personal model that works for the person.

### Slide 2 — More plans are not the same as more connection

> Start with someone who moved to a new city. They meet people and keep saying yes, but still feel disconnected. After a draining night, should they force another event, stay home, or follow up with the one person who felt promising?
>
> Generic advice cannot know. And another chatbot conversation is not the outcome.

### Slide 3 — Wayfinder begins with signals the participant chooses

> Wayfinder begins with signals the participant chooses: a short reflection, a few relationship events, and optional recovery data.
>
> The language model only turns words into inspectable evidence. A separate model updates an uncertain personal state. Every meaningful inference shows its source and can be corrected.

### Slide 4 — One correctable explanation. One bounded next step.

> In this working prototype, the model notices that interest in connection is intact, but capacity is down. Instead of “socialize more,” it offers one bounded next step: protect tonight, then send a low-pressure coffee invitation tomorrow—in your own words.
>
> Nothing is sent automatically. What happened next becomes new evidence.

### Slide 5 — The goal is more human connection, not more AI

> This is not a diagnosis, and the prototype uses synthetic data.
>
> The next milestone is a private longitudinal alpha that remembers consent, corrections, and outcomes across days. The first market is people navigating social transitions and partners who already care about belonging.
>
> The goal is not more time with AI. It is stronger human relationships—and less need for the system over time.

## Live-question answers

### What have you actually built?

> A working Next.js prototype with a typed state-update module and a separate bounded recommendation policy. The pitch scenario uses synthetic wearable and reflection data so the interaction is reliable. The update and action selection are real application logic; the product has not yet demonstrated efficacy with real participants.

### How is this different from ChatGPT?

> ChatGPT generates a plausible response from the current conversation. Wayfinder maintains longitudinal, versioned state; keeps the evidence and uncertainty visible; accepts corrections; and constrains the action policy. The language model is an input adapter, not the source of truth or the decision-maker.

### Does it actually improve human flourishing?

> That is the hypothesis, not a current claim. The prototype proves the interaction and technical boundary. The alpha must first show that people can use it safely across days and that its forecasts beat simple baselines. Any claim that an action helps requires prospective causal evidence.

### Who is the first user?

> Someone navigating a social transition: a first-year student, a person who moved cities, or a new employee. The wedge is narrow because the need is concrete and outcomes can be observed over time.

### Who pays?

> The initial business-model hypothesis is an institution-sponsored product with strict participant ownership—universities or employers can fund access without receiving an individual’s private model or records. Direct consumer subscription remains a second hypothesis. Neither pricing nor channel has been validated yet.

### Why would a university buy it?

> Universities already invest in belonging, retention, and student support, but most tools are surveys, content libraries, or crisis response. Wayfinder is intended to help between those moments with one participant-controlled next step. A pilot would have to demonstrate usefulness without turning private student data into an institutional surveillance product.

### What is the moat?

> Not the chatbot. The defensible asset is a correctable longitudinal model: consented event history, participant-specific baselines, state and model lineage, calibrated uncertainty, and outcome data from bounded real-world actions. Complexity only earns its place if it beats simpler baselines.

### What about privacy?

> Every source is opt-in and revocable. Meaningful inferences retain provenance and can be corrected. The product does not infer another person’s private beliefs, does not send messages on the participant’s behalf, and must support export and deletion. A sponsor never gets the participant’s private model.

### Is this therapy or a medical product?

> No. It is a non-diagnostic social-flourishing product. It does not diagnose, treat, or make clinical claims. High-risk mental-health situations require a separate deterministic protocol rather than improvised model behavior.

### What will prize money or support fund?

> The next participant-visible slice: production authentication, persistent consent and corrections, longitudinal state history, and a small invited alpha. The point is to test the product across days, not add more demo features.

### What would make you stop?

> If the product cannot keep users in control, if its forecasts do not beat simple baselines, if recommendations add burden or harm, or if the longitudinal model adds no measurable value, I simplify or stop. The thesis is designed to fail honestly.
